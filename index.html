Laundry Folding

A human commands the robot at test time through a single sweater folding demonstration (i.e., behavior prompt) collected with bimanual iPhUMI. The robot is trained on three distinct folding tasks and BPP determines which of the training behaviors to follow based on the prompt. All three tasks were seen during training. The purpose of this experiment is to validate that we can command a robot via demonstration to complete complex manipulation tasks.

Failure Cases

While BPP mostly executes the correctly prompted task, we sometimes observe that it executes a different task than specified. For this experiment, the temporal and spatial information in the behavior prompt does not provide any additional task-relevant details: we have very low training task diversity (just three tasks) and all the task-relevant execution information can be memorized in the base model rather than needing to be explicitly referenced from the prompt. This prompt complexity makes the behavior prompt a more indirect identifier of the desired folding task compared to a language command, which can weaken the strength of the task conditioning. We anticipate that behavior prompting would have substantial advantages in a laundry folding setting with many folding tasks and many garmet types where we adapt to a new folding style or garmet at test time. In this case, the spatial and temporal information in the behavior prompt provides useful temporal cues about the folding steps and useful visual cues about interaction points on the new garmet. It would be more challenging to describe these spatial interaction points and folding steps with language conditioning.

DrawAnything

We evaluate behavior prompting on a free-form drawing task where the robot must replicate a novel drawing given a single human demonstration as a behavior prompt. Drawing is well suited for studying behavior prompting as it requires the policy to continuously refernece the prompt to adapt its low-level actions. Results are shown on drawings not seen during training.

LIBERO-Gen

The purpose of the LIBERO-Gen benchmarks is to evaluate test-time adaptation to new tasks. By new tasks we do not mean entirely new action primitives. Instead, we define new tasks as unseen compositions of existing mainpulation primitives. We structure our LIBERO-Gen experiments to evaluate if models can follow new instructions to complete these unseen compositions.

LIBERO-Gen Combination

In this benchmark we have one environment containing two identical bowls that are placed at varying initial locations in the scene. The policy is instructed to pick one of the two bowls and then place that bowl in an instructed target location. We train on many pick-place location combinations and evaluate on unseen pick-place combinations at test-time.

LIBERO-Gen Chain

In this benchmark we have one environment and explore the set of possible two-step interactions. First step actions include opening top/middle drawer, pushing the plate, turning on the stove, and pick-place actions. Second step actions include pick-place actions. We evaluate a model's instruction-following ability to complete sequential execution of two individually seen manipulation primitives never seen jointly during training.

LIBERO-Gen Chain (No Second Step)

We perform an ablation on the training data composition by removing the second-step tasks from the training dataset. This means that when we evaluate on unseen chained tasks, the second step in that chain has not been seen during training. For example, if the unseen chained task is "push the plate to the front of the stove and then put the bowl on the plate," then the policy will have never seen how to place the bowl on the plate after the plate has already moved to the location after the push. This increases the difficulty of completing the unseen tasks as it requires the model to 1) understand new two-step instructions and 2) complete a known manipulation primitive in an unseen environment state.
